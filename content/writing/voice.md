> [!TLDR]
> - Hooks and formats can be copied. Your voice can't, so it's what lasts.
> - AI output always looks polished, so polish tells you nothing about quality.
> - Publish only what you'd sign your name to.
> - Use AI as an editor that checks your drafts against rules, and as a drafter that learns from your edits.

## Your voice is the part nobody can copy

Anyone can copy your format or your hook types. Nobody can copy **how you sound**. That makes voice the one lasting difference between you and everyone posting about the same thing.

- Write like you talk.
- Have opinions.
- Share what actually happened to you.
- Choose how you want to come across (smart, funny, blunt) and stay consistent with it.
- Keep your quirks.
- Don't copy someone else's voice.

## Polish is no longer a signal

AI output always arrives formatted and confident, so looking polished no longer tells you anything. Judge every draft against your own standard: if you wouldn't put your name on it, don't publish it. Addy Osmani says most of their own AI-made creative work never gets shown for this reason.

## Use AI as an editor, not an author

You write the draft; the AI holds it to a set of rules. Load a writing guide into a Claude Project and let it critique your drafts. The guide used in the source is Ryan's [copywriting masterclass](https://x.com/ryangrowthh/status/2118723940638138486), which the earlier lessons in this module are built from. The poster offers no evidence beyond the guide author's own growth.

> [!TRICK] Turn a writing guide into a post editor
> 1. Paste the full guide into a Claude Project.
> 2. Drop in your next 5 drafts.
> 3. Have it rewrite each hook 10 different ways using the guide's rules.
> 4. Make it point out the line where a reader would stop.
> 5. Post the version you'd stop scrolling for yourself.

## Let a drafting bot learn from your edits

If you want AI to write first drafts, keep the last pass for yourself and make the bot learn from it. Each week it compares its drafts with what you published and turns your most frequent edits into new style-guide rules. The author once spent close to half their time rewriting AI output into plain English and is aiming for 20%; they report no result against that target yet.

> [!TRICK] Set up a Writer bot that learns from your edits
> Fill in the brackets and give this to your AI assistant:
>
> ```
> Role: you are my Writer. You draft. I do the last pass.
> Read first: [style guide], [banned-words list], my 10 best [posts / emails] in [folder].
> When you run: whenever I drop an idea or a call note into [Writer inbox doc].
> Done looks like: lines a person would say out loud, zero jargon, at least one number or example taken from my notes, plus 2 options for the first line.
> Every Friday, compare your drafts with what I published. Show me the 5 edits I made most, written as new lines for my style guide.
> Never: invent a result, a client name or a number I didn't give you.
> Ask me first before you: publish, schedule or send anything.
> ```

*Give each bot one job* covers how to set up bots like this one safely.

> [!DO] Write a five-line voice card
> In a note, write: how you want to come across (pick one: smart, funny or blunt), two opinions you hold that others in your field don't, one story that actually happened to you, and one quirk you'll keep. Keep the note open next to your drafts.

> [!CHECK] In writing, why is your voice the lasting difference between you and others posting on the same topic?
> Anyone can copy your hooks and formats, but nobody can copy how you sound.

> [!CHECK] An AI draft looks polished and confident. How do you decide whether to publish it?

> [!CHECK] When you use AI for your writing, what role should it play if you write the drafts yourself?
