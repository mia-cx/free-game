### Why clicks matter right now

First, why this matters so much right now.

Back in 2023, during an antitrust trial against Google, one of Google's vice presidents of Search, Pandu Nayak, testified about a system called NavBoost, which looks at about 13 months worth of click data to see what pages deserve to be at the top.

In other words, clicks are a ranking factor.

> Then in 2024, a batch of Google's internal documents leaked. And in there, you find good clicks, bad clicks, and last longest clicks.

So clicks are actually feedback into how you rank.

And according to the Search Engine Roundtable's report this month, AI Overviews are showing in about eight out of ten searches now.

And Ahrefs found that when AI Overviews show up, the number one result gets about 58% fewer clicks.

And this is where the speed comes in. A new article takes months to tell you if it works. Put a new title on a page that's already ranking, gets picked up the next time Google crawls it.

And within about two weeks, Google Search Console will tell you if the test is working.

So you can run a lot more tests in one year with titles than you can do with about anything else in SEO.

> So tell me if this rings a bell. You have a page that's been sitting in position four or five for months. Google shows it to thousands of people, and almost nobody clicks it.

And then the advice you keep hearing is to write more content, build more backlinks, and wait it out. And that's not your fault, because that's what everyone keeps saying.

> But a lot of the times, the page is fine. The content is fine. The information is fine. Google already likes it enough to put it on page one or two.

It's the title that's losing you the click.

So the first move is not to randomly choose a page and just rewrite the title. It's to choose the pages that matter most, and that's what we're going to cover next.


### Step 1: Find the pages where the title is the problem

So step number one is find the pages where the title is actually the problem.

In Search Console, go to Performance. Turn on all four metrics and go to the Pages tab.

> You're looking for pages between positions three and ten. With at least a couple hundred impressions. And a click-through rate way below what a page at that position normally gets.

And to know what a position normally gets, you need a benchmark. In our experience, position three gets around 9% of the clicks. Position four around 6%, and position five around 4%.

So if you have a page on position four with click-through rate under 1%, okay, that's exactly where you have to begin.

> Inside Distribb, we flag these automatically, and we call these CTR underperformers. It's any page that's ranking in the top ten that's getting 40% fewer than its benchmark.

But that's only half the list. There's a second one hiding one level deeper that most people skip. And it's usually where the easiest wins are.

> It's in the queries, because a page can be fine for its main keyword and gets skipped for searches where it's sitting in positions five or six.

That usually means that the title simply doesn't include the words that people are using to type for that query.


### Step 2: Find out why people skip you

Once you have that list, then you move on to step two, which is to figure out why people are skipping you.

You search for that query in a private window and look at it the way a customer would.

> What is Google actually showing as the title? Because Google rewrites a lot of titles. And you need to know that before you test anything.

You can actually ask Claude to run that search several times until it finds a rewritten title.

> It usually happens when a title is much longer than it should. It's stuffed with keywords or it repeats the same thing on every page. Or it simply doesn't match what the page is about.

And when Google rewrites it, it usually picks your H1 instead. So in other words, you want your H1 and your title saying the same thing.

> Then look at what the pages above you are promising, and what words from the search, the keyword, are you not using.

And this is the thing I promised you at the start. I took 90 days worth of click data for Distribb, and I split it into what the titles actually say.

The pages that include the words that the user is typing, like QuickBooks alternatives, got 4.7% click-through rate. While the pages that only name the category, something like best accounting software, got under 1%.

That's about five times the clicks on the same page.

This is obviously in the case where someone is looking for QuickBooks alternatives.

> Now, before you rush off and rename everything, there's one mistake that you have to avoid, and I'll get to it in step four.


### Step 3: Write the new titles

First, you need the new titles. So step three is to write the new title ideas or ask Claude to write them for you. And I'd always write at least three.

> First, put the keywords that people are using as close to the beginning of the title as you can. If the query is how to get more leads, that's exactly how you wanna begin your title.

Second, make a specific promise. Like seven ways to get more leads or how to get more leads in seven ways.

Third, give people a reason to pick you right now. Like putting the current year.

And pro tip, if you're watching this right now on November 2026, you can actually put the next year, 2027, because nobody has put that yet.

Obviously, don't do this in a January 2027 and put a 2028. This only works in quarter four for any given year because that's when it would actually make sense.

And keep the whole title under 60 characters so Google doesn't cut it off.

> And this is where the bad clicks from that leaked document come back. Don't write a title that the page is not delivering on.

If people click, hate what they read, and bounce, that's a terrible signal for your rankings.


### Step 4: Run the test properly

Now we move on to the next step, which is to run the test properly. Because this is where most people fool themselves.

> You change one thing on one page at a time. And you write down the date and the numbers first, the clicks, the CTR.

And then ask Google to recrawl your page with the URL inspection tool so it notices sooner.

Then you compare the same number of days before and after at a similar position.

If your position moves, then your CTR moves with it. So if you compare a week at position six with a week at position three, the new title will look brilliant even if it did nothing.

And judge the test on clicks, not just click-through rate. Because a new title often gets your page ranking for new long-tail searches.

So your impressions may jump from those new long-tail searches, and your CTR is going to drop while your clicks are actually rising.

So for a busy page, two weeks is actually enough. For quieter pages, just give it a full month.

> And if you have several similar pages, like a page for every location you serve or for every category you have in your products, you can test a title template instead of writing a new title for every single page.

You can change the title pattern on half of them and leave the other half exactly the same. And that's dozens of titles in one single test that you're running.

If it wins, you keep it. If it loses, well, you put back the old one.

And once each ad has about 1,000 impressions or so each, you pick the one that has the highest click-through rate.
