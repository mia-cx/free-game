title: Give strangers a reason to care
summary: Write a launch post that makes strangers stop, reply and quote it.
---

> [!TLDR]
> - Strangers decide in under a second whether to stop. "We're excited to announce" never passes.
> - Stakes, conflict and results that sound too big to be true make people stop.
> - Give strangers something to add: a demo they can examine, dispute or try.
> - Before you write any copy, answer six questions that place your product inside a conversation people already have.

## Strangers decide in under a second

Every launch post has to pass one filter first: **why do I care?** A stranger scrolling past decides in under a second, and announcement language or product detail doesn't survive that.

What fails:

- "We're excited to announce"
- logo animations
- launches of a niche category nobody outside it has heard of
- 12 seconds of a founder talking before anything happens

What stops people: a funding number ("$20M raise" signals it matters), a fight people already have opinions on (datacenters, OpenAI), or a result so big it sounds fake. One launch agency founder, Matt Epstein, puts it plainly: launch comments rarely ask about features. People congratulate the raise or argue about what it means. So decide what people will argue about before you write.

> [!TRICK] Watch people scroll
> Get on a subway or bus and watch people on their phones. Note the moment each one stops on a post, and what the post had. That is the filter your launch has to pass.

## Give strangers something to add

A post spreads through quotes only when someone who doesn't know you has something to say about it. Announcements give them nothing. Concrete material they can examine, dispute or try themselves gives them a reason to quote.

Swap "Excited to announce our new platform" for a specific demo. For example: a messy inventory spreadsheet turning into a working app, showing the input, the output, and what you had to fix by hand. Showing the hand fixes makes it believable and gives people a detail to discuss.

Before you publish, ask: **what could a stranger add to this?** If you can't answer, add something more specific. *Line up your launch-day voices* covers how to seed the angles they might take.

## Plant the narrative before the copy

Start from a conversation people already have, then put your product inside it. Attach the claim to something people already feel, so it reads bigger than the product.

> [!TRICK] Answer six questions before writing launch copy
> 1. What does the market already love?
> 2. What does the market already hate?
> 3. Who is the enemy?
> 4. What is the highest-status outcome?
> 5. What exact words do customers use?
> 6. What is the most polarizing thing you can say that's true?
>
> Example from the source: a hospital intake and notes software launch started as "first step toward medical superintelligence, $24M raised, 10M+ patients". True, but any health-AI company could say it. The rewrite gave four groups a reason to reply: patients who'd been told "it's probably nothing", families who lost someone to a late diagnosis, clinicians, and people who argue about AI. The author's agency reports 1.5M views on day one and a trending launch (their own claim).

Questions 3 and 5 overlap with work you may have done already: see *Say what you are, for whom, against what* and *Research a market in an afternoon*.

## Treat each launch as a small go-to-market

A launch is not one post. Each one needs its own ideal customer, its own channels to reach them, a plan for scaling what works, and a launch video. Kunal Gandhi, a former marketer at Eigen Labs, lists skipping this as a common launch mistake and points to thirdweb's launches as one done well.

A launch is a single moment. Your brand is what people see the rest of the year (see *Build a brand, not a viral hit*). Both matter: the launch gets the spike, the brand keeps the people it brings.

> [!DO] Answer the six questions for your next launch
> Open a doc and write one line for each of the six narrative questions, then one line for "what could a stranger add to this?". Keep it next to you when you write the launch post.

> [!CHECK] What question does every launch post have to answer for a stranger scrolling past?
> "Why do I care?" They decide in under a second, so announcement language like "We're excited to announce" never passes.

> [!CHECK] Your launch post says "Excited to announce our new platform". What do you replace it with?
> A specific demo strangers can examine, dispute or try: the real input, the real output, and what you fixed by hand. That gives them something to add when they quote it.
