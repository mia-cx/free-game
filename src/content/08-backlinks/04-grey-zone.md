> [!TLDR]
> - Paying for roundup spots, swapping links, buying "best X" domains and press-release sites can all work, and can all cost you.
> - Google counts paid links that pass ranking credit, and excessive link exchanges, as link schemes.
> - The exact-match domain story is a single unverified claim, boosted by a domain broker.
> - Decide your line before you're tempted, and write it down.

## Know where Google draws the line

**These tactics sit next to what Google's spam policies call link schemes.** Paid links are allowed only when marked `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`, which stops them passing ranking credit. "Excessive link exchanges" are named as a scheme outright.

None of this means the tactics never work. It means the upside is rankings and visibility, and the downside is links Google treats as spam. Weigh each one on its own.

## Pay for a spot in a roundup

One bootstrapped SaaS builder says the cited sources for buying queries are mostly listicles, and the boring way in is to pay. Others in this course push the earned route instead (see *Give people a reason to link*).

> [!TRICK] The direct paid pitch
> 1. Search on Google or ChatGPT for something your users would ask, where you want to be recommended.
> 2. Click the sources both show. Note the listicles that name competitors but not you.
> 3. Have an agent find the emails of the people running them.
> 4. Email each one. Skip the flattery ("I just saw your blogpost and it really stood out"). Say how you found the list, that you'd like your product in it, and that you're happy to pay.
> 5. Expect some to reject and some to accept. Repeat.
>
> The same author warns against backlink marketplaces: "don't use them."

> [!RISK] Unmarked paid links
> A paid link that passes ranking credit is link spam under Google's policies unless it carries `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`. Cold emails must follow CAN-SPAM and GDPR. Rule for choosing: if you pay, ask for the sponsored label and value the spot for its readers and AI citations, not for ranking credit.

## Swap links inside a relevant network

A link exchange is two sites agreeing to link to each other in their articles. One SEO ranks it as a top-tier tactic, and also sells a link-exchange product, so weigh that ranking accordingly.

> [!TRICK] A relevant editorial exchange
> Join a business network in your field. Match each link to the topic around it, link to the specific answer the reader needs, and log the source and destination URL of every link delivered.

> [!RISK] Excessive exchanges
> Google lists "excessive link exchanges" as a link scheme. Keep any swap rare, relevant and useful to the reader on both sides.

## Buy exact-match "best X" domains

One investor claims that domains matching "best X" searches word for word ranked #1 on Google and in AI answers within 10 days, in the data-labelling niche, and drew a seven-figure acquisition offer. No URLs, traffic or rankings were shown, and a domain broker amplified the post.

> [!TRICK] The claimed playbook
> 1. Find a niche with heavy spending, urgent need, high ticket sizes, and buyers and sellers who struggle to find each other. The author's example: buying and selling AI training data.
> 2. Register domains that match queries one to one: "best data labs", "best ai data labs", "best data labelling", "best data labelling for ai", "best data labelling platform", "best data labelling software", "best data labelling tools", "best data brokers", "best enterprise fine tuning services".
> 3. Publish relevant content on them, consistently tie the entity to those keywords, and wait.
>
> The logic: when one lead is worth a lot, owning the "best X" answer is worth renting or selling.

> [!RISK] Exact-match domains can fade
> Google demoted low-quality exact-match domains in 2012, so results may not last. The method's details ("lexical proximity", "entity mapping") are vague. Treat this as a bet with domain fees as the stake, not a plan.

## Use press-release sites only for real news

Press-release sites host your announcement; they are not news coverage. Use them only for a genuine launch, study or milestone.

> [!TRICK] Press-release sites
> PRLog, openPR, PR.com, IssueWire, 1888PressRelease, Express Press Release, Pressbox, PressReleaseBox, PressReleasePoint, Press Release Power, PRFree, Circle Acts, Press Release Submit, PR News Releaser, SubmitPR. Some limit free publication or clickable links.

> [!RISK] Little SEO value
> A hosted release carries little SEO value and is not independent coverage. Don't count it as press, and don't pay for distribution expecting rankings.

> [!DO] Write your link policy
> In your marketing doc, write one line per tactic above: paid roundups, link exchanges, exact-match domains, press releases. Mark each "yes", "no" or "only if", with your reason.

> [!CHECK] When does Google allow a paid link?
> Only when it's marked `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`, so it passes no ranking credit. An unmarked paid link counts as link spam.

> [!CHECK] A roundup owner will add your product for a fee. If you pay, what do you ask for, and what are you buying?
> Ask for the sponsored label. You're buying the spot's readers and AI citations, not ranking credit.

> [!CHECK] Why treat buying exact-match "best X" domains as a bet rather than a plan?
> The evidence is one unverified claim, amplified by a domain broker. Google also demoted low-quality exact-match domains in 2012, so results may not last.
